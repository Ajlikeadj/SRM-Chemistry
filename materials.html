S1 · Lecture

Magnetic materials

Magnetism comes from electrons: each has a spin magnetic moment (and orbital motion adds a little). Atoms with all electrons paired have no net moment; atoms with unpaired electrons are tiny magnets. How these moments behave together gives five types. The magnetization M of a material in a field H is measured by the susceptibility χ = M/H.

  1. Diamagnetic.All electrons paired (Cu⁺, Zn²⁺, water, Bi). The field induces a weak opposing moment, so χ is small and negative, and the material is slightly repelled.
  2. Paramagnetic.Unpaired electrons, but the moments point randomly due to thermal motion. A field aligns a few, so χ is small and positive (Curie law χ = C/T). The spin-only moment is μ = √(n(n+2)) μB, where n is the number of unpaired electrons: Cu²⁺ (n = 1) gives 1.73 μB; high-spin Fe³⁺ (n = 5) gives √35 = 5.92 μB.
  3. Ferromagnetic.Strong coupling aligns neighbouring moments in the same direction within regions called domains (Fe, Co, Ni). χ is very large, and the material keeps magnetization after the field is removed. Above the Curie temperature T_C (Fe 1043 K, Co 1388 K, Ni 627 K) heat destroys the order and the material becomes paramagnetic: χ = C/(T − T_C).
  4. Antiferromagnetic.Neighbouring moments point in opposite directions and cancel (MnO, Cr). Order disappears above the Néel temperature.
  5. Ferrimagnetic.Opposing moments of unequal size leave a net moment (magnetite Fe₃O₄, ferrites MFe₂O₄). They are magnetic but insulating, so they are useful at high frequency.

The hysteresis loop

Magnetizing a ferromagnet, reversing the field and repeating traces a loop of M against H. Remanence is the magnetization left at H = 0; coercivity is the reverse field needed to bring M to zero; the loop area is energy lost as heat per cycle.

Soft magnets: narrow loop, low coercivity, low loss → transformer cores, inductors, electromagnets (silicon steel, soft ferrites) Hard magnets: wide loop, high coercivity, strong remanence → permanent magnets, motors, speakers (Nd₂Fe₁₄B, SmCo₅, alnico), hard-disk media
S2 · Lecture

Superconducting materials

In 1911 Kamerlingh Onnes found that mercury’s resistance dropped abruptly to zero at 4.2 K. Below a critical temperature T_c a superconductor has two defining properties.

  1. Zero resistance.A current in a superconducting ring flows for years without any measurable decay.
  2. Meissner effect.The material expels magnetic field from its interior (perfect diamagnetism, χ = −1). This is different from a merely perfect conductor and is what makes magnets levitate above a superconductor.

Superconductivity is destroyed if T > T_c, if the magnetic field exceeds a critical field H_c, or if the current exceeds a critical current. Approximately H_c(T) = H_c(0)[1 − (T/T_c)²].

Type I and Type II

Type I (pure metals such as Hg, Pb): the superconducting state collapses abruptly at H_c. Type II (alloys and ceramics: Nb₃Sn, YBCO): between H_c1 and H_c2 the field penetrates in thin tubes (vortices) while the rest remains superconducting, so they tolerate very high fields and are the ones used in practice.

BCS theory (low-temperature superconductors)

  1. An electron passing through the lattice pulls the positive ions slightly toward it.This leaves a region of extra positive charge.
  2. A second electron is attracted to that region.Two electrons with opposite momentum and spin form a bound Cooper pair (via lattice vibrations, phonons).
  3. Pairs behave as bosons.They condense into one coherent quantum state that flows without scattering. An energy gap Δ ≈ 1.76 k_B T_c separates the paired state from ordinary electrons, so small vibrations cannot break it.

Supporting evidence is the isotope effect, T_c ∝ M^(−1/2): heavier isotopes of the lattice have lower T_c. High-T_c cuprates are not fully explained by BCS.

Hg 4.2 K Nb 9.2 K NbTi 9.5 K Nb₃Sn 18 K MgB₂ 39 K YBa₂Cu₃O₇ (YBCO) 92 K Hg-cuprates ~133 K

YBCO works above the boiling point of liquid nitrogen (77 K), which is cheap. Applications: MRI magnets (NbTi), the LHC accelerator magnets, maglev trains, SQUID magnetometers, lossless power cables and fault-current limiters. The main limitation is the cost of cooling and the brittleness of ceramics.

S3 · Tutorial

Band structure of solids and the role of doping

How bands form

Take Unit 1’s idea: two atomic orbitals combine into a bonding and an antibonding MO. With N atoms, N orbitals combine into N molecular levels spread over an energy width of a few eV. In a solid N ≈ 10²³, so the levels are so closely spaced that they form a continuous band. Each atomic level (3s, 3p …) becomes its own band, and gaps between bands are energies with no allowed states. Bands made from the occupied orbitals are the valence band (VB); those from empty ones are the conduction band (CB); the gap is E_g.

Metals, insulators, semiconductors

Metal: band partly filled (Na, 3s half full) or VB and CB overlap (Mg). Electrons can move at once → high conductivity. Insulator: VB full, CB empty, E_g large (> ~4 eV; diamond 5.5 eV). No carriers. Semiconductor: same picture but small E_g (Ge 0.67, Si 1.12, GaAs 1.42 eV). Heat or light can excite electrons.

The Fermi level is the highest occupied energy at 0 K. When an electron jumps to the CB it leaves a hole in the VB, and both carry current. Temperature raises conductivity of a semiconductor but lowers it in a metal (more lattice scattering).

Doping

Adding a trace of a different element (one atom in 10⁶ to 10⁹) greatly changes conductivity.

  1. n-type.Phosphorus (5 valence electrons) replaces a Si atom (4). Four electrons bond; the fifth is loosely held and sits in a donor level about 0.045 eV below the CB, so it is excited into the CB even at room temperature. Majority carriers: electrons.
  2. p-type.Boron (3 valence electrons) leaves a missing bond, a hole. An acceptor level just above the VB captures VB electrons, leaving mobile holes. Majority carriers: holes.
  3. Quantitative.Mass-action law: n·p = nᵢ². Pure Si at 300 K has nᵢ ≈ 10¹⁰ cm⁻³. For 10¹⁶ cm⁻³ of P: n ≈ 10¹⁶ and p = (10¹⁰)²/10¹⁶ = 10⁴ cm⁻³. Conductivity:
    σ = q(nμₑ + pμₕ) = (1.6×10⁻¹⁹)(10¹⁶)(1350) ≈ 2.2 S/cm (electron mobility of Si ≈ 1350 cm²/V s)
    which is about 10⁵ times that of pure Si.
S6 · Lecture

Semiconductor materials

Intrinsic semiconductors are pure; their conductivity depends only on thermal excitation across the gap, with the number of carriers given by

n ∝ exp(−E_g / 2kT) σ = σ₀ exp(−E_g / 2kT)

Extrinsic semiconductors are doped. Common materials: Si and Ge (elemental); GaAs, InP, GaN (III–V compounds); CdS, ZnO (II–VI).

Direct and indirect gaps

In a direct semiconductor (GaAs, GaN) the CB minimum is directly above the VB maximum, so an electron falls back and emits a photon efficiently, which is why they are used in LEDs and lasers. Si is indirect, so recombination mainly produces heat, which makes Si poor for light emission but still the standard for chips and solar cells. The emitted wavelength follows λ(nm) = 1240/E_g(eV); GaN (3.4 eV) emits in the near-UV/blue, which led to white LED lighting.

The p–n junction

  1. Join p- and n-type.Electrons diffuse from n to p and holes from p to n, leaving fixed charged ions behind.
  2. A depletion region forms.It has an internal electric field that stops further diffusion.
  3. Forward bias (p to +).The barrier shrinks and current flows. Reverse bias widens the barrier and almost no current flows, so the junction is a one-way valve (a diode).
  4. Devices.LED (recombination emits light), solar cell (light creates pairs which the field separates), transistor (two junctions, a switch and amplifier).

Making semiconductors: silicon is purified by zone refining to better than one impurity in 10⁹, grown as single crystals (Czochralski method), and then doped by diffusion or ion implantation.

S7 · Lecture

Introduction to nanomaterials

Nanomaterials have at least one dimension between 1 and 100 nm. For scale: a human hair is about 80,000 nm wide, a DNA helix 2 nm, a hydrogen atom 0.1 nm. A nanometre is one billionth of a metre.

Why small is different

Two effects switch on at this size. (1) Surface effects: a large fraction of the atoms sit on the surface, and these are less strongly bonded and more reactive. (2) Quantum effects: electrons are confined, so energy levels and the band gap depend on size (see quantum dots).

How they are made

Top-down: start from bulk and break it down: ball milling, lithography, etching, laser ablation Bottom-up: build from atoms and molecules: sol-gel, co-precipitation, chemical vapour deposition, hydrothermal, self-assembly

Bottom-up chemistry gives better control of size and uniformity. In solution, a capping agent (citrate, thiols, polymers) sticks to the particle surface to stop growth and prevent clumping.

S8 · Tutorial

Properties of nanomaterials

  1. Surface-to-volume ratio.For a sphere SA/V = 4πr²/(4/3 πr³) = 3/r, so it rises as r falls. Roughly, with atoms 0.3 nm across, the fraction of atoms at the surface is 1 − ((r − 0.3)/r)³:
    10 nm particle (r = 5 nm): ≈ 17 % of atoms on the surface 3 nm particle (r = 1.5 nm): ≈ 49 % of atoms on the surface
    Consequence: much higher catalytic activity per gram.
  2. Melting point.Surface atoms are less tightly bound, so the melting point falls with size (gold nanoparticles of a few nm melt far below the bulk 1064 °C).
  3. Optical.Gold nanoparticles look red, not gold: conduction electrons oscillate collectively with light (surface plasmon resonance, near 520 nm for ~20 nm spheres). Changing size or shape shifts the colour. Semiconductor nanocrystals change colour through quantum confinement.
  4. Electrical.Confinement quantizes energy levels, so conductance changes in steps and the band gap grows as particles shrink.
  5. Magnetic.Below ~20 nm, magnetic particles (Fe₃O₄) become single-domain and superparamagnetic: strongly magnetic in a field, but with no remanence when it is removed, so they do not clump.
  6. Mechanical.Smaller grains in a metal block dislocation motion, so strength rises as grain size falls (Hall–Petch relation).
S11 · Lecture

Types of nanomaterials

By dimension

0D all three dimensions nano: nanoparticles, quantum dots, fullerene C₆₀ 1D two dimensions nano: nanotubes, nanowires, nanorods 2D one dimension nano: graphene, MoS₂ sheets, thin films 3D bulk with nano features: nanocomposites, nanoporous materials

Fullerene C₆₀: 60 carbon atoms in a football shape (12 pentagons, 20 hexagons), about 0.7 nm across. Carbon nanotubes: graphene sheets rolled into cylinders (single- or multi-walled). The way the sheet is rolled, labelled by (n, m), decides the electrical behaviour: metallic if n − m is divisible by 3, otherwise semiconducting. They are extremely strong along their length and conduct heat well. Graphene: a single layer of carbon atoms in a hexagonal lattice, very strong, transparent and highly conductive, but with zero band gap.

By composition

Carbon-based (above); metals (Au, Ag, Pt); metal oxides (TiO₂, ZnO, Fe₃O₄); semiconductor nanocrystals (CdSe, InP); polymeric and lipid nanoparticles (liposomes, dendrimers); ceramic.

S12 · Lecture

Quantum dots

Quantum dots are semiconductor nanocrystals of about 2–10 nm. They are so small that the electron and hole created by light are squeezed into a space smaller than they would naturally occupy in bulk (the exciton Bohr radius). This is quantum confinement.

The particle-in-a-box connection

From Unit 1, a confined particle has energies proportional to 1/L². Shrinking the crystal raises the confinement energy of both electron and hole, so the gap grows. The Brus equation expresses this:

E_g(R) ≈ E_g(bulk) + (ħ²π²/2R²)(1/mₑ + 1/mₕ) − 1.8 e²/(4πε₀εR) ^ confinement, grows as 1/R² ^ Coulomb attraction, smaller correction

Result: one material, many colours. CdSe has a bulk gap of 1.74 eV (about 710 nm, red/infrared). Smaller dots emit green or blue, larger dots red, simply by changing size. For emission at 2.48 eV, λ = 1240/2.48 = 500 nm. Dots have narrow, tunable emission, high brightness and good stability.

Uses: QLED displays and televisions, bio-imaging labels, LEDs, solar cells and photodetectors. The 2023 Nobel Prize in Chemistry honoured their discovery and synthesis. Concern: Cd and Pb are toxic, so cadmium-free dots (InP) and carbon dots are being developed.

S13 · Tutorial

Applications of nanomaterials

  1. Medicine.Liposomes and polymer particles carry drugs to targets; lipid nanoparticles deliver mRNA vaccines; iron-oxide particles enhance MRI; gold nanoshells heat and destroy tumour cells under near-infrared light.
  2. Electronics.Transistors with nanometre features, flash memory, flexible conductive inks, nanowire sensors.
  3. Energy.Nanostructured electrodes for Li-ion batteries, nanocatalysts in fuel cells, quantum dot and perovskite solar cells.
  4. Environment.TiO₂ photocatalysts break down pollutants, nanomembranes filter water, nanoscale iron treats contaminated groundwater.
  5. Consumer and materials.ZnO/TiO₂ transparent sunscreens, silver antibacterial fabrics, self-cleaning glass, nano-silica in tyres, stronger lighter composites.
  6. Risks.Particles small enough to enter cells or be inhaled may behave differently from the bulk material, so toxicity and environmental fate must be tested, and release should be controlled.
S4–S5 · Practice

Practical: strength of an acid with a pH meter

A glass electrode’s potential changes by 0.0592 V per pH unit (Unit 3), so a pH meter turns a voltage into pH after calibration.

  1. Calibrate.Using pH 4.00, 7.00 and 10.00 buffers; rinse the electrode with distilled water between solutions.
  2. Titrate.Put 25 mL of acid in a beaker, add 0.1 M NaOH from the burette in 1 mL steps (0.2 mL near the jump), stir, and record pH after each addition.
  3. Find the end point.Plot pH vs V, and also ΔpH/ΔV vs V; the peak is the end point.
  4. Calculate.
    N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ (acid = NaOH) Example: 25 mL HCl, 22.5 mL of 0.1 N NaOH: N = 0.1×22.5/25 = 0.090 N Strength = N × equivalent mass = 0.090 × 36.46 = 3.28 g/L

Strong vs weak acid curves. A strong acid (HCl) starts at pH ≈ 1 and rises steeply through pH 7 at the end point. A weak acid (CH₃COOH) starts higher, has a flatter buffer region, and its end point is above 7 (about 8.7) because the acetate formed is basic. At the half-equivalence point [HA] = [A⁻], so by the Henderson–Hasselbalch equation

pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]) → pH(half-equivalence) = pKa acetic acid: pKa = 4.76, Ka = 1.8×10⁻⁵
S9–S10 · Practice

Practical: band gap of a semiconductor

For an intrinsic semiconductor R = R₀ exp(E_g/2kT). Taking logs gives a straight line:

ln R = ln R₀ + (E_g/2k)(1/T)
  1. Measure.Heat a semiconductor sample (Ge crystal or a thermistor) slowly in an oil bath; record its resistance and temperature at regular steps, with the thermometer touching the sample.
  2. Convert and plot.T in kelvin; plot ln R against 1/T.
  3. Slope.m = E_g/2k, so E_g = 2k·m. With k = 8.617×10⁻⁵ eV/K:
    E_g (eV) = 1.723×10⁻⁴ × m(K) Example: m = 3900 K → E_g = 0.67 eV (germanium)
  4. Check.Use only the high-temperature (intrinsic) region; at low T, impurity levels dominate and the slope is smaller. Errors: lag between the thermometer and the sample, and self-heating from the measuring current.

An optical alternative (Tauc plot from UV–Vis absorption) appears in Unit 5.

S14–S15 · Practice

Practical: HCl and acetic acid mixture by conductometry

Conductance G = 1/R measures how well ions carry current. Each ion contributes in proportion to its concentration and its mobility. Ionic conductivity (S cm² mol⁻¹): H⁺ 350, OH⁻ 199, Na⁺ 50, Cl⁻ 76, CH₃COO⁻ 41. H⁺ and OH⁻ are unusually mobile.

Titrating the mixture with NaOH

  1. Strong acid first.HCl is neutralized: H⁺ (350) is replaced by Na⁺ (50), so conductance falls steeply.
  2. Then the weak acid.After HCl is gone, acetic acid (barely ionized) turns into sodium acetate (a salt with Na⁺ and acetate ions), so conductance rises slowly.
  3. Excess base.Once both acids are neutralized, free OH⁻ (199) accumulates and conductance rises sharply.

Plot conductance (corrected for dilution) against NaOH volume; three straight lines meet at two break points. V₁ is the end of HCl, V₂ the end of acetic acid.

N(HCl) = N(NaOH) × V₁ / V(sample) N(CH₃COOH) = N(NaOH) × (V₂ − V₁) / V(sample) Example: 0.1 N NaOH, V₁ = 12.0 mL, V₂ = 20.0 mL, 25 mL sample: HCl = 0.1×12.0/25 = 0.048 N acetic acid = 0.1×8.0/25 = 0.032 N

Practical tips: keep the temperature constant, stir, use a more concentrated titrant so the volume change is small, and rinse the cell. An advantage of this method is that it needs no indicator, so it works for coloured or turbid solutions, and the break points are found from the graph rather than a colour change.
