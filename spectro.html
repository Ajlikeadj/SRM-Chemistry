S1 · Lecture

Electromagnetic radiation

Spectroscopy studies how matter absorbs, emits or scatters electromagnetic radiation. Radiation is an oscillating electric and magnetic field travelling at c = 3.00×10⁸ m/s. It is described by wavelength λ (distance between peaks), frequency ν (oscillations per second), and wavenumber ν̃ = 1/λ (waves per cm, the unit used in IR). It is also made of photons, each of energy E = hν.

c = λν E = hν = hc/λ ν̃ = 1/λ E(eV) ≈ 1240/λ(nm)

Example, green light of 500 nm: ν = 3.00×10⁸ / 5.00×10⁻⁷ = 6.0×10¹⁴ Hz; E = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(6.0×10¹⁴) = 3.98×10⁻¹⁹ J = 2.48 eV = 239 kJ/mol, comparable to the energy of a chemical bond. This is why light can drive chemistry.

Regions of the spectrum and what each does to a molecule

Radio waves (60–900 MHz) flip nuclear spins → NMR Microwave change molecular rotation → microwave spectroscopy Infrared (4000–400 cm⁻¹) change bond vibration → IR Visible/UV (700–200 nm) excite valence electrons → UV–Vis X-ray (< 10 nm) excite or remove inner-shell electrons → XPS, X-ray

Higher frequency means higher energy. In an experiment, a spectrum is a plot of how much radiation is absorbed (absorbance) against wavelength, frequency or wavenumber. A molecule absorbs only when the photon energy matches an energy gap in the molecule exactly (ΔE = hν), so the spectrum is a fingerprint of its energy levels. Transmittance T = I/I₀ and absorbance A = −log₁₀T are the two ways of reporting it.

S2 · Lecture

Vibrational and electronic transitions

A molecule’s energy is quantized in three ways, with very different sizes:

E_total ≈ E_electronic + E_vibrational + E_rotational electronic ≈ 1–10 eV (UV–Vis) vibrational ≈ 0.05–0.5 eV (IR, 400–4000 cm⁻¹) rotational ≈ 0.001 eV (microwave)

Vibrational levels

A bond behaves like a spring with levels E_v = (v + ½)hν, v = 0, 1, 2, … The lowest level has the zero-point energy ½hν. At room temperature kT = 0.0259 eV ≈ 208 cm⁻¹, so the fraction of C=O bonds (1700 cm⁻¹) in v = 1 is e^(−1700/208) ≈ 3×10⁻⁴: almost every molecule starts in v = 0, and the main IR absorption is v = 0 → 1.

Electronic levels

Absorbing a UV or visible photon moves an electron from an occupied orbital (usually the HOMO) to an empty one (LUMO). Each electronic state has its own set of vibrational levels, so a UV band is broad: electronic change plus vibrational changes at once.

Selection rules

Not every energy match leads to absorption. IR: the vibration must change the molecule’s dipole moment (HCl yes; N₂ and O₂ no). Electronic: spin must not change (ΔS = 0), and transitions that are symmetry-forbidden (such as n → π* in carbonyls) are weak. NMR: only nuclei with spin ≠ 0. Selection rules explain why some absorptions are strong, others weak, and others missing.

S3 · Tutorial

The Born–Oppenheimer approximation

The full Schrödinger equation for a molecule has electrons and nuclei all moving and interacting, which is impossible to solve exactly except for the simplest cases. The Born–Oppenheimer (BO) approximation simplifies it using one fact: a proton is about 1836 times heavier than an electron (and other nuclei are heavier still).

  1. Compare speeds.Nuclei move slowly; electrons move so fast that they adjust immediately to any change in the nuclear positions. Compare a heavy truck with a swarm of bees around it.
  2. Freeze the nuclei.Treat the nuclear positions R as fixed numbers and solve the electronic Schrödinger equation (electron kinetic energy, electron–nucleus attraction, electron–electron repulsion, plus the fixed nuclear repulsion):
    Ĥ_el ψ_el(r; R) = E_el(R) ψ_el(r; R)
  3. Repeat for many geometries.Plotting E_el(R) against the nuclear separation gives a potential energy curve (surface for bigger molecules), with a minimum at the equilibrium bond length.
  4. Let the nuclei move on that curve.E_el(R) acts as the potential energy in a second, nuclear Schrödinger equation, whose solutions are the vibrational and rotational levels.
  5. Result.
    ψ_total ≈ ψ_el(r; R) × ψ_nuclear(R) E_total = E_el + E_vib + E_rot

Consequences. (1) Energy separates into electronic, vibrational and rotational parts, which is what lets spectroscopy treat each separately. (2) The concept of a molecule having a shape and a bond length exists because of the potential energy surface. (3) Computational chemistry (Unit 2 tools) calculates E_el(R) to optimize geometries.

The Franck–Condon principle

Absorption of a photon takes about 10⁻¹⁵ s, far shorter than a vibration (10⁻¹³ s). The nuclei have no time to move, so the transition is “vertical” on the potential energy diagram. The upper-state vibrational level reached is the one whose wavefunction overlaps best with the ground state, which creates the vibrational fine structure in UV spectra.

Where it fails

When two electronic surfaces come close or cross (photochemistry, conical intersections), electron and nuclear motion are coupled. It is also less accurate for very light nuclei (hydrogen tunnelling).

S6 · Lecture

UV–visible spectroscopy

Beer–Lambert law, derived

  1. Light is absorbed layer by layer.A thin slice dl of solution of concentration c absorbs a fraction of the light proportional to c·dl:
    dI/I = −k c dl
  2. Integrate from 0 to l.
    ln(I₀/I) = k c l → log₁₀(I₀/I) = (k/2.303) c l
  3. Name the terms.
    A = ε c l
    A is absorbance, ε the molar absorptivity (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹), c concentration (mol/L), l path length (cm).

Example: A = 0.45 at λmax for ε = 15 000, cell 1.00 cm: c = 0.45/(15 000 × 1) = 3.0×10⁻⁵ M. Transmittance T = 10^(−0.45) = 35.5 %. The law holds for dilute solutions (A below about 1) and a single absorbing species.

Electronic transitions in molecules

σ → σ* < 200 nm alkanes (far UV, not seen) n → σ* ~ 180–250 O, N, S lone pairs (alcohols, amines) π → π* 170–250+ alkenes, aromatics; strong (ε 10³–10⁵); moves to longer λ as conjugation grows n → π* 270–300 C=O, N=O; weak (ε 10–100), symmetry-forbidden

Chromophore: the part of a molecule that absorbs (C=C, C=O, benzene ring). Auxochrome: a group (–OH, –NH₂) that shifts and intensifies absorption. Shifts: bathochromic (red, to longer λ), hypsochromic (blue), hyperchromic (more intense), hypochromic (less).

Why conjugation lowers the energy (particle-in-a-box check)

Model the π electrons of a polyene as N electrons in a 1-D box of length L (Unit 1). The HOMO is n = N/2 and LUMO n = N/2 + 1, so

ΔE = (N + 1) h² / 8mL² Butadiene: N = 4, L ≈ 5.6 Å: ΔE = 5(6.626×10⁻³⁴)² / [8(9.109×10⁻³¹)(5.6×10⁻¹⁰)²] = 9.6×10⁻¹⁹ J = 6.0 eV λ = 1240 / 6.0 ≈ 207 nm (observed 217 nm)

As the chain lengthens, L grows and N grows, but L² grows faster, so ΔE falls, and λ shifts to the red: ethylene 165 nm, butadiene 217, hexatriene 258, octatetraene 290, β-carotene (11 C=C) about 450 nm, so it absorbs blue and looks orange. The colour we see is the complement of the colour absorbed.

Solvent and instrument

Polar solvents stabilize the n-orbital, so n → π* shifts to shorter λ (blue), while π → π* usually moves slightly red. Instrument path: lamp (deuterium for UV, tungsten for visible) → monochromator → sample cuvette (quartz for UV) → detector. Always record a blank of the solvent first.

S7 · Lecture

Infrared spectroscopy

Hooke’s law: vibrational frequency of a bond

  1. Model.Two atoms joined by a spring of force constant k (N/m). The restoring force is F = −kx.
  2. Equation of motion.For two masses, replace them by the reduced mass μ = m₁m₂/(m₁ + m₂). Newton’s law μ d²x/dt² = −kx has oscillating solutions with
    ν = (1/2π)√(k/μ) ν̃ = (1/2πc)√(k/μ) ν̃(cm⁻¹) = 130.3 √(k(N/m) / μ(amu))
  3. Meaning.Stiffer bonds (large k) and lighter atoms (small μ) vibrate faster.
HCl: k = 516 N/m, μ = (1×35)/36 = 0.972 amu → ν̃ = 130.3√(516/0.972) = 3002 cm⁻¹ (observed fundamental 2886) DCl: μ = 1.892 amu → 130.3√(516/1.892) = 2152 cm⁻¹ (isotope shift, ratio √(0.972/1.892) = 0.72) C–C 450, C=C 960, C≡C 1560 N/m with μ = 6 → 1128, 1648, 2101 cm⁻¹

Higher bond order means a higher frequency. Real bonds are slightly anharmonic: levels crowd together at high v and the bond can dissociate; this allows weak overtones (Δv = ±2) and makes the observed fundamental lower than the harmonic value.

Which vibrations appear?

Dipole must change. A non-linear molecule has 3N − 6 vibrational modes (H₂O: 3: symmetric stretch, asymmetric stretch, bend); a linear one has 3N − 5 (CO₂: 4). The symmetric stretch of CO₂ gives no dipole change and is IR-inactive, while its asymmetric stretch (2349 cm⁻¹) is strongly active, which is how CO₂ traps heat in the atmosphere.

Group frequencies (cm⁻¹)

O–H alcohol 3200–3600 broad O–H acid 2500–3300 very broad N–H amine 3300–3500 C–H sp³ 2850–3000 C–H sp² / aromatic 3000–3100 C≡C or C≡N 2100–2260 C=O ketone, aldehyde 1700–1730 strong C=O ester 1735–1750 C=C 1620–1680 C–O 1050–1300 Below 1500: fingerprint region, unique to each molecule

How to read a spectrum

  1. Look at 3600–2500.Broad band = O–H; two spikes = NH₂; 3000 vs 2900 separates sp² from sp³ C–H.
  2. Check 2300–2000for triple bonds.
  3. Check 1800–1650for a strong C=O. Position tells the type.
  4. Use the fingerprint regionto confirm by matching with a reference.

Examples: acetone shows one strong peak near 1715 cm⁻¹; ethanol shows a broad 3300 and a C–O near 1050; a carboxylic acid combines a very broad 2500–3300 with 1710. Practical: FTIR with a KBr pellet, a thin liquid film, or ATR (a crystal pressed against the sample, minimal preparation).

S8 · Tutorial

Band gap from UV–visible spectra (Tauc plot)

For semiconductors and nanoparticles (Unit 4), UV–Vis gives the band gap. Absorption begins when photon energy exceeds E_g, because an electron is promoted from VB to CB. The Tauc relation describes the absorption edge:

(α h ν)^(1/γ) = B (h ν − E_g) γ = 1/2 for direct allowed gaps (plot (αhν)²), γ = 2 for indirect gaps (plot (αhν)^½)
  1. Record the spectrumof the sample (a thin film, or a dispersion with a solvent blank).
  2. Convert wavelength to energy.
    hν (eV) = 1240 / λ (nm)
  3. Convert absorbance to absorption coefficient.
    α = 2.303 A / t (t = film thickness or cell path)
    For a dispersion, A itself can be used for a qualitative edge.
  4. Plot (αhν)² against hν.The curve rises steeply above the edge and has a straight section.
  5. Extrapolate the straight section to (αhν)² = 0.The intercept on the energy axis is E_g.

Example: ZnO nanoparticles with an absorption edge near 380 nm: E_g ≈ 1240/380 = 3.26 eV (bulk ZnO is about 3.3 eV, direct). Smaller particles show an edge at shorter wavelength, which shows the quantum-confinement blue shift of Unit 4.

S11 · Lecture

Nuclear magnetic resonance: introduction

Many nuclei have spin, so they behave as tiny magnets. ¹H, ¹³C, ¹⁹F and ³¹P have spin I = ½ (two states, m = +½ and −½). ¹²C and ¹⁶O have I = 0 and are silent. Without a field the two states have equal energy; in a strong magnetic field B₀ they split:

ΔE = γħB₀ = hν Larmor frequency ν = γB₀/2π ¹H: γ = 2.675×10⁸ rad s⁻¹ T⁻¹. At B₀ = 7.05 T: ν = (2.675×10⁸)(7.05)/2π = 3.0×10⁸ Hz = 300 MHz

Resonance: a radio-frequency pulse with exactly this frequency flips nuclei from the lower (aligned) state to the upper. Magnets are labelled by their ¹H frequency (300, 400, 600 MHz). Population difference: ΔN/N ≈ hν/2kT = (6.626×10⁻³⁴)(3×10⁸)/(2 × 1.381×10⁻²³ × 298) ≈ 2.4×10⁻⁵, only about 24 per million, so NMR is a low-sensitivity technique and needs many scans or strong magnets.

How a modern spectrometer works

  1. Place the sample in a strong superconducting magnet.(Unit 4: a Nb–Ti solenoid, cooled by liquid helium.)
  2. Send a short radio pulsethat excites all nuclei at once.
  3. Record the decaying signalfrom the nuclei as they relax (the free-induction decay, FID).
  4. Fourier-transform the FIDinto a spectrum of intensity against frequency.

Samples are dissolved in deuterated solvents (CDCl₃, D₂O) so that the solvent gives no ¹H signal. Tetramethylsilane (TMS) is the reference.

S12 · Lecture

NMR explained: chemical shift, integration and splitting

1. Chemical shift: what kind of environment?

Electrons around a nucleus circulate in the field and create a small opposing field, so the nucleus feels B_eff = B₀(1 − σ), where σ is the shielding. Nuclei with more electron density are more shielded and resonate at lower frequency. To make the scale independent of magnet strength, shifts are reported relative to TMS:

δ (ppm) = (ν_sample − ν_TMS) / ν_spectrometer × 10⁶ TMS: δ = 0

Example: a peak 1500 Hz from TMS on a 300 MHz instrument has δ = 1500/300 = 5.0 ppm (on a 600 MHz one it would be 3000 Hz but still 5.0 ppm). Electronegative neighbours remove electrons (deshielding) and increase δ.

¹H: R–CH₃ 0.9 –CH₂– 1.3 C=C–CH₃ 1.7 CH₃–C=O 2.1 N–CH 2.3–3 O–CH 3.3–4.5 alkene C–H 4.6–6 aromatic 6.5–8 aldehyde 9–10 carboxylic acid 10–12

2. Integration: how many hydrogens?

The area under each signal is proportional to the number of hydrogens giving it. Ratios, such as 3 : 2 : 3, are read directly.

3. Splitting (spin–spin coupling): what are the neighbours?

A hydrogen feels the small magnetic fields of H atoms on adjacent carbons (three bonds away), which splits its signal into n + 1 lines when there are n equivalent neighbours. Intensities follow Pascal’s triangle. The spacing is the coupling constant J (Hz), the same for the two coupled partners.

0 neighbours: singlet (s) 1: doublet 1:1 2: triplet 1:2:1 3: quartet 1:3:3:1 4: quintet

Equivalent hydrogens do not split each other, and O–H protons usually appear as a broad singlet because they exchange rapidly.

Examples

Ethanol CH₃CH₂OH: CH₃ δ 1.2 triplet, 3H (two CH₂ neighbours) CH₂ δ 3.7 quartet, 2H (three CH₃ neighbours) OH δ ~2.6 broad singlet, 1H Ethyl acetate: CH₃C=O δ 2.0 s 3H; OCH₂ δ 4.1 q 2H; CH₃ δ 1.25 t 3H

¹³C NMR

Only 1.1 % of carbon is ¹³C, so the signals are weak, but the spectra are simple: with proton decoupling, every different carbon gives one line. The number of lines equals the number of distinct carbon environments (ethanol 2; benzene 1). Shifts: alkyl 10–50, C–O 50–90, alkene and aromatic 110–150, C=O 160–220 ppm.

S13 · Tutorial

Applications of NMR, with a worked problem

Solving a structure: C₄H₈O₂

  1. Degrees of unsaturation.(2C + 2 − H)/2 = (10 − 8)/2 = 1, so there is one C=O or one ring.
  2. Read the ¹H spectrum.Signals: δ 1.25 (triplet, 3H), δ 2.0 (singlet, 3H), δ 4.1 (quartet, 2H). Total 8H matches the formula.
  3. Assign.A triplet 3H next to a quartet 2H is an ethyl group, CH₃–CH₂–. The quartet at 4.1 is far downfield, so the CH₂ is attached to oxygen: –O–CH₂CH₃. The 3H singlet at 2.0 has no neighbours and sits at the value for CH₃–C=O.
  4. Assemble.CH₃C(=O)–O–CH₂CH₃, ethyl acetate. Confirm with IR (strong C=O near 1740 cm⁻¹, no O–H).

Wider uses

Structure determination in organic and natural-product chemistry; purity and quantification (peak area is directly proportional to the number of nuclei); reaction monitoring; pharmaceuticals and drug screening; protein and nucleic-acid structure with multi-dimensional NMR; metabolomics. MRI is NMR of water protons: magnetic field gradients label position, and relaxation times differ among tissues, giving images without ionizing radiation.

How the three techniques complement each other

UV–Vis: conjugation and concentration IR: functional groups NMR: complete carbon–hydrogen skeleton (together they identify an unknown)
S4–S5 · Practice

Practical: Hooke’s-law IR spectra simulation

Aim: predict the IR frequency of diatomic bonds from k and μ, and draw a simulated spectrum.

  1. Collect data.
    k (N/m): HF 966 HCl 516 HBr 412 CO 1857 C–H ~500
  2. Compute μ in amu,then ν̃ = 130.3 √(k/μ). Examples: HF (μ = 0.957) gives 4140; CO (μ = 6.857) gives 2145 cm⁻¹ (observed 2143).
  3. Isotope effect.Replace H with D: μ almost doubles, so ν̃ falls by about 1/√2 (HCl 3002 → DCl 2152).
  4. Plot.Draw each absorption as a Lorentzian peak:
    import numpy as np, matplotlib.pyplot as plt x = np.linspace(500, 4500, 4000) peaks = [130.3*np.sqrt(k/mu) for k, mu in [(966,.957),(516,.972),(1857,6.857)]] y = sum(1/(1+((x-p)/15)**2) for p in peaks) plt.plot(x, y); plt.gca().invert_xaxis() # IR spectra are plotted high→low
  5. Compare.Harmonic values overestimate real frequencies by a few percent because of anharmonicity.
S9–S10 · Practice

Practical: excitation energy of an organic molecule

Aim: compute the first electronic excitation energy of a dye or conjugated molecule and compare with its colour.

  1. Build and optimize.Draw the molecule (Avogadro, RDKit) and optimize the geometry with a quantum method (for example B3LYP/def2-SVP in ORCA or Psi4).
  2. Run a TD-DFT calculationfor the lowest 5–10 excited states.
  3. Read the output.For each state: excitation energy E (eV), oscillator strength f (intensity; ignore f ≈ 0 states), and which orbitals are involved (usually HOMO → LUMO).
  4. Convert.
    λ (nm) = 1240 / E (eV)
  5. Compare with experimentand with the colour seen: a molecule absorbing 450 nm (blue) appears yellow-orange.
  6. Study a trend.Do ethylene → butadiene → hexatriene → octatetraene and watch λ increase (165, 217, 258, 290 nm), as the box model predicts.

Note: the HOMO–LUMO gap is only a rough guide; true excitation energies also include the attraction between the excited electron and the hole it leaves, so they differ from orbital-energy gaps. Calculated λ often differs from experiment by 10–30 nm.

S14–S15 · Practice

Viva: questions and answers

  1. Why must photon energy equal the energy gap?Energy levels are quantized, so only ΔE = hν is absorbed.
  2. Which transition does IR cause, and which UV–Vis?IR: vibrational (v = 0 → 1). UV–Vis: electronic (HOMO → LUMO).
  3. Why is N₂ IR-inactive?Its stretching vibration does not change the dipole moment (zero before and after).
  4. What does the Born–Oppenheimer approximation assume?Nuclei are far heavier and slower, so electronic and nuclear motion can be separated.
  5. State the Beer–Lambert law.A = εcl: absorbance is proportional to concentration and path length.
  6. Why do longer conjugated systems absorb at longer wavelength?The HOMO–LUMO gap shrinks (box length grows).
  7. In Hooke’s law, what happens to ν̃ when the bond is stiffer, or atoms heavier?Stiffer: increases. Heavier: decreases (μ in the denominator).
  8. Why use TMS in NMR?Single sharp signal at δ 0, inert, volatile, and its protons are highly shielded.
  9. What is the n + 1 rule?A signal coupled to n equivalent neighbouring H splits into n + 1 lines.
  10. Why is NMR less sensitive than UV–Vis?The energy gap is tiny, so the population difference between spin states is only ~ 10⁻⁵.
  11. How do you find a band gap from UV–Vis?Tauc plot: (αhν)² against hν; extrapolate the straight part to the energy axis.
  12. How does an ethyl group appear in ¹H NMR?A triplet (3H) and a quartet (2H).
