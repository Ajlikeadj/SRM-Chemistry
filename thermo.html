Basic terms and the four laws

Thermodynamics tracks energy changes in bulk matter. We first fix the vocabulary.

Basic terms

System: the part of the universe we study. Surroundings: everything else. Boundary: what separates them. Open system: exchanges matter and energy (boiling pot). Closed: exchanges energy only (sealed flask). Isolated: exchanges neither (ideal thermos). Homogeneous: one phase with uniform properties (salt solution). Heterogeneous: more than one phase (ice in water).

Extensive properties depend on amount (mass, volume, U, H, S, G); intensive ones do not (T, P, density). A state function depends only on the present state, not the path, so its change is final − initial. Heat q and work w are path functions. Processes: isothermal (T constant), isobaric (P), isochoric (V), adiabatic (q = 0), and reversible (infinitesimally slow, always at equilibrium).

The laws

  1. Zeroth law.If A and B are each in thermal equilibrium with C, they are in equilibrium with each other. This defines temperature and makes thermometers possible.
  2. First law.Energy is conserved. Using the convention that q and w are positive when they enter the system:
    ΔU = q + w
  3. Second law.The entropy of an isolated system (or of system + surroundings) never decreases: ΔS(universe) ≥ 0. Heat does not flow spontaneously from cold to hot.
  4. Third law.The entropy of a perfect crystal is zero at 0 K, so absolute entropies can be tabulated.
S1 · Lecture

Thermodynamic functions: internal energy and enthalpy

Internal energy U is the total kinetic and potential energy of the molecules in the system. Only changes ΔU can be measured. It changes through heat and work.

Work of expansion

A gas pushing against an external pressure does work w = −P_ext ΔV (negative: the system loses energy). For a reversible isothermal expansion of an ideal gas, integrating dw = −P dV with P = nRT/V:

w = −nRT ln(V₂/V₁)

Example: 1 mol expands isothermally at 298 K to double volume: w = −(1)(8.314)(298)(0.693) = −1.72 kJ. Since U of an ideal gas depends only on T, ΔU = 0 and q = +1.72 kJ.

Enthalpy

  1. At constant volumeno expansion work, so ΔU = q_v. Heat measured in a bomb calorimeter is ΔU.
  2. At constant pressureΔU = q_p − PΔV. Define enthalpy H = U + PV. Then ΔH = ΔU + PΔV = q_p. Heat measured in an open beaker is ΔH.
  3. Relating them for gases:
    ΔH = ΔU + Δn(g)·RT
    Example: N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ has Δn = −2, so ΔH is lower than ΔU by 2RT ≈ 4.96 kJ at 298 K.

Heat capacity

C_V = (∂U/∂T)_V and C_P = (∂H/∂T)_P. For one mole of ideal gas H = U + RT, so differentiating with respect to T gives

C_P − C_V = R

Hess’s law and enthalpies of formation

Because H is a state function, the enthalpy of a reaction is the same whether done in one step or several.

ΔH°rxn = ΣΔH°f(products) − ΣΔH°f(reactants) CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O(l): = −393.5 + 2(−285.8) − (−74.8) = −890.3 kJ/mol

Entropy: meaning and estimation

Energy conservation cannot explain why processes have a direction. Entropy does.

Two definitions

Thermodynamic (Clausius): heat absorbed reversibly divided by temperature. Statistical (Boltzmann): S = k ln W, where W is the number of microscopic arrangements (microstates) matching the macroscopic state. More ways to arrange energy and particles means higher entropy, which is why gases > liquids > solids and mixtures > pure substances.

dS = q_rev / T S = k ln W k = 1.381×10⁻²³ J/K

How to estimate ΔS

  1. Phase change (T constant).
    ΔS = ΔH(transition)/T
    Ice melting: 6010 / 273.15 = 22.0 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Water boiling: 40660 / 373.15 = 109 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Vaporization is larger because the gas has far more microstates.
  2. Heating at constant pressure.Using dq_rev = nC_P dT and integrating:
    ΔS = nC_P ln(T₂/T₁)
    Example: 1 mol of water from 298 to 348 K, C_P = 75.3 J/K: ΔS = 75.3 × ln(348/298) = 11.7 J/K.
  3. Ideal gas expansion (isothermal).
    ΔS = nR ln(V₂/V₁)
    Doubling the volume of 1 mol: 8.314 × 0.693 = 5.76 J/K.
  4. Chemical reaction.
    ΔS°rxn = ΣS°(products) − ΣS°(reactants)
    Absolute S° values exist because of the third law. Sign guide: more gas moles after reaction means ΔS > 0.

Entropy of the universe

A spontaneous process has ΔS_univ = ΔS_sys + ΔS_surr > 0. Heat given to the surroundings at constant pressure is −ΔH_sys, so ΔS_surr = −ΔH_sys/T, which sets up free energy.

Tutorial: enthalpy estimation from bond energies

Bonds broken (energy in) minus bonds formed (energy out). CH₄ + 2O₂ → CO₂ + 2H₂O(g):

Broken: 4(C–H 413) + 2(O=O 498) = 2648 kJ Formed: 2(C=O 799) + 4(O–H 463) = 3450 kJ ΔH ≈ 2648 − 3450 = −802 kJ/mol (tabulated: −802.3)
S2, S6 · Lecture

Gibbs free energy: derivation and use

  1. Start from the second law.ΔS_univ = ΔS_sys + ΔS_surr > 0 for a spontaneous process.
  2. Insert ΔS_surr = −ΔH_sys/T (constant T and P):
    ΔS_univ = ΔS_sys − ΔH_sys/T
  3. Multiply by −T and name the result.
    −TΔS_univ = ΔH − TΔS_sys ≡ ΔG G = H − TS
    So ΔG = −TΔS_univ, and a spontaneous change at constant T and P has ΔG < 0. ΔG = 0 means equilibrium.

Signs: ΔH < 0 and ΔS > 0 means spontaneous at all T. ΔH > 0 and ΔS < 0 means never. ΔH < 0, ΔS < 0 is spontaneous at low T. ΔH > 0, ΔS > 0 is spontaneous at high T (crossover T = ΔH/ΔS).

Free energy and equilibrium

ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q At equilibrium ΔG = 0 and Q = K: ΔG° = −RT ln K

Large negative ΔG° means K ≫ 1. Also, ΔG equals the maximum useful (non-expansion) work obtainable: w_max = ΔG, the link to batteries below. Estimation: ΔG°rxn = ΣΔG°f(products) − ΣΔG°f(reactants), or ΔH° − TΔS°.

Case study: ammonia synthesis (Haber–Bosch)

N₂ + 3H₂ ⇌ 2NH₃
  1. ΔH°.−92.2 kJ (exothermic).
  2. ΔS°.2(192.5) − 191.6 − 3(130.7) = −198.7 J/K (4 gas moles become 2).
  3. ΔG° at 298 K.
    −92.2 − 298(−0.1987) = −33.0 kJ K = e^(33000/(8.314×298)) ≈ 6×10⁵
    Very favourable at room temperature.
  4. Crossover temperature.
    T = ΔH/ΔS = 92200/198.7 ≈ 464 K
    Above this ΔG° > 0. At 700 K: ΔG° = −92.2 + 139.1 = +46.9 kJ and K ≈ 3×10⁻⁴.

Why the plant uses 400–500 °C anyway: at room temperature the rate is essentially zero. High T gives speed but lowers the equilibrium yield (Le Chatelier). The compromise: ~450 °C, 150–300 atm (high P favours fewer gas moles) and an iron catalyst. A catalyst speeds the approach to equilibrium but does not change ΔG° or K. Unreacted gas is recycled, and NH₃ is condensed out. Practice task: compute ΔG° and K at several temperatures and plot them.

Free energy and EMF of cells

A galvanic cell separates a spontaneous redox reaction into two half-cells so that electrons flow through an external wire. Anode: oxidation (negative terminal). Cathode: reduction (positive). A salt bridge completes the circuit. Daniell cell notation: Zn | Zn²⁺ (1 M) ‖ Cu²⁺ (1 M) | Cu.

Anode: Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ E° = −0.76 V (as reduction) Cathode: Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu E° = +0.34 V E°cell = E°(cathode) − E°(anode) = 0.34 − (−0.76) = 1.10 V

Link between ΔG and E

  1. Electrical work.Moving n moles of electrons through potential E does work nFE (F = 96485 C/mol, Faraday constant).
  2. Maximum work equals ΔG.
    ΔG = −nFE ΔG° = −nFE°
    Daniell: ΔG° = −2(96485)(1.10) = −212 kJ. Spontaneous cell means E > 0.
  3. Equilibrium constant.Combine ΔG° = −RT ln K with ΔG° = −nFE°:
    E° = (RT/nF) ln K = (0.0592/n) log K (298 K)
    Daniell: log K = 2(1.10)/0.0592 = 37.2, so K ≈ 10³⁷.

Standard potentials are measured against the standard hydrogen electrode (Pt | H₂ 1 bar | H⁺ 1 M), defined as 0 V. A more positive E° means a stronger oxidizing agent.

S11, S13 · Lecture & Tutorial

The Nernst equation and electrode potentials

  1. Start with the free energy of a reaction.
    ΔG = ΔG° + RT ln Q
  2. Replace ΔG with −nFE.
    −nFE = −nFE° + RT ln Q
  3. Divide by −nF.
    E = E° − (RT/nF) ln Q E = E° − (0.0592/n) log Q at 25 °C

Example: Daniell cell with [Zn²⁺] = 0.01 M and [Cu²⁺] = 1 M: Q = 0.01, so E = 1.10 − (0.0592/2) log(0.01) = 1.10 + 0.059 = 1.159 V. As the reaction proceeds, Q → K and E → 0 (a dead battery).

Tutorial: electrode potential expressions

For a half-reaction Ox + ne⁻ → Red the potential is E = E° − (0.0592/n) log([Red]/[Ox]). Pure solids and liquids count as 1.

Metal–metal ion, Mⁿ⁺ + ne⁻ → M: E = E° + (0.0592/n) log[Mⁿ⁺] Hydrogen, 2H⁺ + 2e⁻ → H₂ (1 bar): E = −0.0592 pH Ag/AgCl, AgCl + e⁻ → Ag + Cl⁻: E = 0.222 − 0.0592 log[Cl⁻] Calomel (sat. KCl): E ≈ 0.241 V (reference electrode) Redox, Pt | Fe³⁺, Fe²⁺: E = 0.77 + 0.0592 log([Fe³⁺]/[Fe²⁺]) Glass electrode: E = const − 0.0592 pH

Concentration cells

Same electrode, different concentrations: E° = 0, so E = (0.0592/n) log(c₂/c₁). The cell runs until the two concentrations equalize, a pure entropy-driven process.

Applications

pH measurement; finding K and K_sp from E°; potentiometric titrations; batteries and fuel cells; corrosion (rusting is a galvanic process in which iron is the anode); electroplating and electrolysis (the reverse process, needing E > E°cell).

S14–S15 · Practice

Potentiometric titration of Fe²⁺ with K₂Cr₂O₇

Cr₂O₇²⁻ + 14H⁺ + 6Fe²⁺ → 2Cr³⁺ + 6Fe³⁺ + 7H₂O
  1. Set up.Pt indicator electrode and a calomel reference in the acidified Fe²⁺ sample. Add dichromate in small portions and record the potential after each.
  2. What happens.Before the end point E = 0.77 + 0.0592 log([Fe³⁺]/[Fe²⁺]), which rises slowly. At the equivalence point nearly all Fe²⁺ is gone and E jumps sharply towards the dichromate couple (E° = 1.33 V).
  3. Find the end point.Plot E vs V, then ΔE/ΔV vs V (peak at the end point), or Δ²E/ΔV² (crosses zero).
  4. Calculate.
    N₁V₁ = N₂V₂ (equivalent weight of K₂Cr₂O₇ = M/6)
    The Fe²⁺ concentration follows from the equivalence volume. Advantage over an indicator: works with coloured or turbid solutions.
