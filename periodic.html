Effective nuclear charge and Slater’s rules

In a many-electron atom, an outer electron is attracted by the nucleus (+Z) but repelled by the other electrons, which partly cancel the attraction. This is shielding. The net pull actually felt is the effective nuclear charge:

Zeff = Z − S (S = shielding or screening constant)

Slater gave simple rules to estimate S, so Zeff can be calculated for any electron.

Slater’s rules

  1. Write the configuration in groups.(1s)(2s,2p)(3s,3p)(3d)(4s,4p)(4d)(4f)(5s,5p)… Electrons to the right of the one considered do not shield it.
  2. For an s or p electron (ns, np):
    Same group: 0.35 each (0.30 for 1s) Shell n − 1: 0.85 each Shell n − 2 and lower: 1.00 each
  3. For a d or f electron (nd, nf):
    Same group: 0.35 each All groups to the left: 1.00 each
  4. Add up S and compute Zeff = Z − S.

Worked examples

Na, 3s electron (Z = 11; 1s² 2s²2p⁶ 3s¹) S = 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 8.8 Zeff = 2.2 Cl, 3p electron (Z = 17; 1s² 2s²2p⁶ 3s²3p⁵) S = 6(0.35) + 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 10.9 Zeff = 6.1 Zn (Z = 30), 4s electron: S = 0.35 + 18(0.85) + 10(1.00) = 25.65 Zeff = 4.35 Zn (Z = 30), 3d electron: S = 9(0.35) + 18(1.00) = 21.15 Zeff = 8.85

What they show. Across a period (Na 2.2 → Cl 6.1), Zeff rises because each new proton adds +1 but each new electron in the same shell shields only 0.35, so atoms shrink and hold electrons more tightly. Down a group, Zeff stays roughly constant but the outer electron sits in a larger shell, so size grows. In Zn the 3d electron feels more charge than the 4s, which is why 4s electrons are lost first when transition metals ionize.

Slater’s effective principal quantum number n* (n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 → n* = 1, 2, 3, 3.7, 4.0, 4.2) allows a rough energy: E = −13.6 (Zeff/n*)² eV.

S2 · Lecture

Ionization energy, electron affinity, electronegativity, polarizability

Ionization energy (IE)

Energy needed to remove the outermost electron from a gaseous atom: M(g) → M⁺(g) + e⁻. It rises with Zeff and falls with distance. First IE (eV) for period 2:

Li 5.39 Be 9.32 B 8.30 C 11.26 N 14.53 O 13.62 F 17.42 Ne 21.56

General rise: Zeff increases. B below Be: B’s electron is in a 2p orbital, higher in energy and shielded by 2s. O below N: O has a paired 2p electron, and pair repulsion makes it easier to remove. Down a group (Li 5.39, Na 5.14, K 4.34 eV): larger radius, weaker pull. Successive IEs always increase, with a huge jump after the valence shell is emptied. Mg: 738, 1451, then 7733 kJ/mol, which shows that Mg is +2.

Electron affinity (EA)

Energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron: X(g) + e⁻ → X⁻(g). Large negative values (energy released) are found for halogens. Cl (−349 kJ/mol) is more negative than F (−328) because F is so small that the extra electron suffers strong repulsion. N is near zero (the new electron must pair in a half-filled p subshell) and noble gases are positive (new electron needs a new shell).

Electronegativity (χ)

The ability of an atom in a bond to attract the shared electrons. Pauling: derived from bond energies (F 3.98, O 3.44, Cl 3.16, N 3.04, C 2.55, H 2.20, Na 0.93). Mulliken: the average of IE and EA. Allred–Rochow: based on Zeff and covalent radius r.

χ(Mulliken) = (IE + EA)/2 χ(A–R) = 0.359 Zeff/r² + 0.744

Use: a difference above about 1.7 suggests ionic bonding; 0.4 to 1.7 polar covalent; below 0.4 nearly pure covalent (C–H).

Polarizability (α)

How easily the electron cloud is distorted by an electric field (induced dipole μ = αE). It grows with atomic volume (≈ r³) and with the number of loosely held electrons, so it increases down a group and decreases across a period. Consequences: stronger London dispersion forces, hence higher boiling points down the noble-gas group (He 4 K, Ar 87 K, Xe 165 K), and by Fajans’ rules a large, easily polarized anion (I⁻) combined with a small, highly charged cation gives a more covalent bond.

S3 · Tutorial

Tutorial: Zeff problems with solutions

  1. C 2p electron (Z = 6; 1s² 2s²2p²).S = 3(0.35) + 2(0.85) = 2.75, so Zeff = 3.25.
  2. F 2p electron (Z = 9).S = 6(0.35) + 2(0.85) = 3.80, so Zeff = 5.20. Compare with C: this is the reason F is much smaller and has a larger IE.
  3. Mg 3s electron (Z = 12).S = 0.35 + 8(0.85) + 2(1.00) = 9.15, so Zeff = 2.85.
  4. K 4s electron (Z = 19).S = 8(0.85) + 10(1.00) = 16.8, so Zeff = 2.2; with n* = 3.7 this explains why K’s IE is lower than Na’s.
  5. Method.Write the configuration → group the orbitals → pick the right rule (s/p vs d/f) → count → Zeff = Z − S → compare with neighbours.

Check yourself: predict which of O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ has the largest radius. All have 10 electrons, so the one with the smallest Z has the smallest Zeff, and O²⁻ is largest.

S4–S5 · Practice

Practical: hardness of water by EDTA titration

Idea. Hardness comes from dissolved Ca²⁺ and Mg²⁺ salts. EDTA (H₄Y) is a six-point (hexadentate) chelating agent that wraps around a metal ion and binds it 1:1 whatever the charge: M²⁺ + H₂Y²⁻ → MY²⁻ + 2H⁺. Because H⁺ is released, the titration is carried out in a pH 10 buffer (NH₃/NH₄Cl).

  1. Indicator.Eriochrome Black T (EBT) is blue when free at pH 10, and wine-red when bound to Ca²⁺/Mg²⁺. EDTA binds the metal more strongly than EBT does, so at the end point it takes the metal from EBT and the colour changes from wine red to blue.
  2. Standardize EDTA.Titrate a known solution (e.g. standard CaCO₃ dissolved in HCl, or ZnSO₄) to find its exact molarity.
  3. Titrate the water.Pipette 50 mL of water, add 2 mL buffer and a few drops of EBT, and titrate with EDTA until the colour just turns blue. Repeat until concordant (±0.1 mL).
  4. Calculate.
    Hardness (ppm as CaCO₃) = M(EDTA) × V(EDTA, mL) × 100.09 × 1000 / V(sample, mL) Example: 0.01 M × 10.0 mL × 100.09 × 1000 / 50 mL = 200 ppm

Interpretation (approximate, WHO): under 60 ppm soft; 60–120 moderate; 120–180 hard; over 180 very hard. Temporary hardness (bicarbonates) is removed by boiling; permanent hardness (chlorides, sulfates) needs softening such as ion exchange. Sources of error: wrong pH, missing the faint end point, and traces of Fe or Cu that block the indicator.

S6 · Lecture

Basics of cheminformatics

Cheminformatics applies computer science to chemical problems: storing structures, searching them, calculating properties, and predicting behaviour of molecules without having to make each in the lab. A typical question: “out of 10 million candidate molecules, which few are likely to bind this protein and not be toxic?”

Molecule → computer-readable representation → descriptors → model → prediction → experiment

The key idea is that a molecule is a graph: atoms are nodes, bonds are edges, and labels carry atom type and bond order. Most algorithms work on this graph or numbers derived from it.

Descriptors

Numbers that describe a molecule: molecular weight, logP (oil/water preference), hydrogen-bond donors (HBD) and acceptors (HBA), polar surface area (TPSA), rotatable bonds, ring counts. Lipinski’s rule of five (oral drug-likeness): MW ≤ 500, logP ≤ 5, HBD ≤ 5, HBA ≤ 10. Aspirin (MW 180, HBD 1, HBA 4) passes easily.

Where it is used

Virtual screening and docking, QSAR (quantitative structure–activity relationships), ADMET prediction (absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion, toxicity), reaction and retrosynthesis planning, and materials discovery. Similar structure often means similar property (the “similarity principle”), which is the foundation of the whole field.

S7, S8 · Lecture & Tutorial

Representing molecules in two and three dimensions

Ways to represent one molecule

Molecular formula (C₂H₆O) is ambiguous: ethanol and dimethyl ether both match. A structural graph fixes connectivity. A connection table lists atoms and bonds; the adjacency matrix for the heavy atoms of ethanol (C₁–C₂–O₃) is

C1 C2 O3 C1 [ 0 1 0 ] C2 [ 1 0 1 ] O3 [ 0 1 0 ]

Text strings (SMILES, InChI), bit-vector fingerprints (does the molecule contain substructure X?) and 3D coordinates complete the list. Fingerprint similarity uses the Tanimoto coefficient T = c/(a + b − c) where a and b are the bits set in each molecule and c those set in both; 1 is identical, 0 is no overlap.

Why 3D matters

Binding, reactivity and spectra depend on shape: enantiomers have identical 2D graphs but different 3D shapes (and different biological effects). Flexible molecules exist as conformers by rotating about single bonds (torsion angles), and software searches for the lowest-energy ones using a force field such as MMFF94.

Coordinates and geometry

A 3D structure is a list of atoms with x, y, z in ångströms (XYZ format: atom count, title, then one line per atom). Bond lengths and angles are computed from coordinates:

d = √((x₂−x₁)² + (y₂−y₁)² + (z₂−z₁)²) cos θ = (u·v)/(|u||v|) where u and v are the bond vectors from the central atom

Example, water: O (0, 0, 0), H₁ (0.757, 0.586, 0), H₂ (−0.757, 0.586, 0).

d(O–H) = √(0.757² + 0.586²) = 0.957 Å u·v = −0.573 + 0.343 = −0.230, |u||v| = 0.916 cos θ = −0.251 → θ = 104.5°

Other 3D formats: MOL/SDF (coordinates plus bonds), MOL2, PDB (proteins). Internal coordinates (Z-matrix) describe geometry through bond lengths, angles and torsions instead. Practice (S9–S10): sketch a molecule, export a 2D MOL file, generate a 3D conformer, optimize it, and measure one bond length and one angle.

S11 · Lecture

The SMILES format

SMILES (Simplified Molecular Input Line Entry System) writes a molecular graph as a short text string by walking through the atoms.

  1. Atoms.Organic-subset atoms (B, C, N, O, P, S, F, Cl, Br, I) are written plainly, with hydrogen counts filled in from normal valence. Others go in brackets: [Na+], [NH4+], [O-], [13C].
  2. Bonds.Single is implied (or −), double =, triple #, aromatic bonds are implied between lowercase atoms.
  3. Branches.Parentheses: CC(C)C is isobutane.
  4. Rings.Match digits: the two atoms labelled 1 are bonded. C1CCCCC1 is cyclohexane; benzene is c1ccccc1 (lowercase = aromatic).
  5. Stereo.@ and @@ give chirality (viewed from the first neighbour, the others go anticlockwise or clockwise); / and \ give double-bond E/Z.
  6. Disconnected parts.A dot, e.g. [Na+].[Cl-].
methane C ethanol CCO acetic acid CC(=O)O acetonitrile CC#N acetone CC(=O)C L-alanine N[C@@H](C)C(=O)O paracetamol CC(=O)Nc1ccc(O)cc1 aspirin CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O caffeine Cn1cnc2c1c(=O)n(C)c(=O)n2C

Reading acetic acid CC(=O)O: C (methyl) → C (carbonyl carbon) with a branch (=O) double-bonded oxygen → O (hydroxyl). Non-uniqueness: CCO and OCC are both ethanol. Software applies a canonicalization algorithm so each molecule has one canonical SMILES, which is crucial for finding duplicates in databases. InChI is a related standard identifier that is unique by design.

S12 · Lecture

Standard software tools

RDKit (open-source Python/C++): reads SMILES and files, draws structures, calculates descriptors and fingerprints, performs substructure search and generates 3D conformers. Open Babel: converts between over 100 chemical file formats. Avogadro, PyMOL, ChimeraX: build and visualize in 3D. MarvinSketch, ChemDraw: drawing. AutoDock Vina: docking of ligands into proteins. ORCA, Gaussian: quantum-chemical calculations. scikit-learn, PyTorch: machine-learning models.

from rdkit import Chem from rdkit.Chem import Descriptors, AllChem m = Chem.MolFromSmiles('CC(=O)Oc1ccccc1C(=O)O') # aspirin print(Descriptors.MolWt(m)) # 180.16 mh = Chem.AddHs(m) AllChem.EmbedMolecule(mh) # make 3D coordinates AllChem.MMFFOptimizeMolecule(mh) # relax with force field # Open Babel, command line: obabel -:"CCO" -O ethanol.sdf --gen3d
S13 · Tutorial

Chemical databases and how to use them

PubChem: tens of millions of compounds with structures, properties and bioassays (aspirin is CID 2244). ChEMBL: curated bioactivity of drug-like molecules against targets. ZINC: purchasable compounds, ready-made for docking. DrugBank: approved drugs and targets. PDB: 3D structures of proteins and complexes. CSD: small-molecule crystal structures. NIST WebBook: thermochemical and spectral data.

  1. Search by:name, formula, SMILES or InChI, substructure (contains this ring), or similarity (Tanimoto above a threshold).
  2. Downloadas SDF or CSV for analysis.
  3. Clean the data.Standardize SMILES, strip salts and solvents, merge duplicates, make units consistent, and check outliers. Poor data gives poor models, regardless of the algorithm.
  4. Cite the source and its version.Databases are updated over time.
S14–S15 · Practice

Practical: AI classification model for drug toxicity

Goal: given a molecule, predict whether it is toxic. This is binary classification, trained on molecules with known labels (for example the public Tox21 data, about 8,000 compounds over 12 assays).

  1. Collect and clean.Load SMILES and labels, standardize, remove duplicates and invalid structures.
  2. Featurize.Morgan (circular) fingerprints: each atom’s neighbourhood up to a radius (usually 2) is hashed to a position in a 2048-bit vector. Each molecule becomes a row of 0s and 1s. Alternative: RDKit descriptors or a graph neural network on the graph itself.
  3. Split.Train/test (e.g. 80/20). A scaffold split puts different ring-system families in train and test, which gives an honest estimate for new chemistry.
  4. Train.Logistic regression, random forest or gradient boosting.
  5. Evaluate.
    precision = TP/(TP+FP) recall = TP/(TP+FN) ROC-AUC: 0.5 = random guessing, 1.0 = perfect
    Toxic molecules are rare, so accuracy alone misleads: a model that says “non-toxic” always can score 90%. Use recall, precision and AUC.
  6. Interpret and limit.Inspect which bits matter (they map back to substructures, such as nitro groups and reactive electrophiles). A model is reliable only for molecules similar to its training data, the applicability domain. It cannot replace experiments.
X = [AllChem.GetMorganFingerprintAsBitVect(m, 2, 2048) for m in mols] X_tr, X_te, y_tr, y_te = train_test_split(X, y, test_size=0.2, stratify=y) clf = RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=300).fit(X_tr, y_tr) print(roc_auc_score(y_te, clf.predict_proba(X_te)[:, 1]))
